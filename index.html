Rules briefing

RL Racing League

Train a reinforcement-learning driver for a simulated car. Race it on tracks nobody has seen. The simulator that trains it also judges it.

MuJoCo MJXBrax PPO4 GPUs per teamlap time is the score
Welcome. This deck covers four things: what the league is, enough RL vocabulary to follow along, the contest rules, and the car with its observation vector. The background is the actual car model driving a real benchmark track, rendered live.

What you are building

  • A driver: a neural-network policy that reads 60 numbers 50 times a second and outputs steering and throttle.
  • A car setup: 13 tunable physical parameters (wheelbase, tyre friction, torque, ...) that travel with your submission.
  • Everything runs in MJX, MuJoCo on JAX: thousands of cars simulate in parallel on the GPUs, so a training run takes minutes.
  • You edit one directory, team/. The platform, the physics and the judge are frozen.
Local numbers transfer: rlr evaluate and the organizer judge run the same code on the same tracks.
The loop every driver lives in: observation in, action out, reward back.
Two deliverables: a policy and a car configuration. Stress that the evaluation tool teams run locally is literally the judge, so there is no surprise at scoring time.

How the league runs

Three phases. Stage 1 is a two-hour sprint on public tracks. Overnight is for tuning and longer training. Stage 2 moves to hidden tracks and adds the multi-car finale.

Stage 1 (2 hours)

Goal

Fastest lap on the three public benchmark tracks. Everyone drives the same baseline car.

Tracks

oval 449 m, constant 10 m half-width. loop 285 m, gentle corners. serpent 382 m, 6.4 to 9.1 m half-width, corners down to 12.5 m radius.

Workflow

rlr train → rlr evaluate → rlr package → rlr submit --stage 1. The baseline config laps in about 5 minutes of training.

Finale

A ghost-mode grid race on serpent: everyone's solo run replayed together, so you can see who is quickest where.

Submit early. Each new zip is judged in about 40 s of wall clock, and the leaderboard keeps your best.
The three tracks are public and ship in the image, so teams can overfit to them if they wish. That is fine for Stage 1; it stops working in Stage 2.

Overnight (tune and retrain)

  • Tune the car. 13 scalar parameters plus the wheel collision shape. Values are never rejected, only clamped into the allowed ranges.
  • Train on more tracks. The generator makes unlimited easy, medium and hard circuits from a seed; the default mix is 256 tracks.
  • Shape the reward. team/reward.py is yours. Progress per step is the default signal.
  • Optional self-play. A race: block puts frozen opponents in the world so your policy learns the opponent slots of the observation.

Compute per team

One g4dn.12xlarge per team: 4 × NVIDIA T4 GPUs, 48 vCPUs. Expect about 80k environment steps per second solo with physical walls; the 60 M-step baseline takes about 13 minutes. Four cars in one world drop that to roughly 18k steps per second.

Keep the poll loop in mind

The organizer judge keeps running overnight, so late Stage 1 submissions still score.

Overnight is where most of the gains come from: longer training, harder generated tracks, and a car setup that fits the driving style the policy learned.

Stage 2 (hidden tracks)

Hidden tracks

Generated from the same generator with a secret seed, two per difficulty preset, plus hand-tuned circuits. Expect hairpins and chicanes you have not seen. Corners are never tighter than about 10 m radius and half-widths never under 6 m.

Primary score: ghost mode

Same solo judging as Stage 1, summed over the hidden tracks, replayed together as a ghost race on the hardest one.

Finale: collision mode

Every submission in one world with real car-to-car contact. Grid start, 240 s, first to 3 laps wins.

Generalisation is the whole game in Stage 2. A policy that memorised the oval will meet its first hairpin on live television.
Hidden tracks come from the same distribution as the training generator, so training on a wide mix of generated tracks is the intended path to a good Stage 2 result.

Reinforcement learning
in five minutes

Just enough vocabulary to read the config file and the training curves.

Section two: RL basics. Keep it practical and tied to the racing setting.

Agent, environment, observation, action, reward

Agent. The driver: a policy π that maps an observation to an action.

Environment. The MJX world: car, track, walls, physics. It owns the true state.

Observation oₜ. What the agent is allowed to see: 60 floats, track- and car-relative.

Action aₜ. Two numbers in [-1, 1]: steer and throttle. Applied for 4 physics substeps (20 ms).

Reward rₜ. A scalar after every step. The agent's only notion of good and bad.

Step. One observe → act → simulate cycle, 50 per simulated second.

The environment keeps the full MuJoCo state; the agent sees only the observation. Reward is a design choice made by the team; lap time is the league's choice and the two are only loosely coupled.

Policy, episode, return, discount

Policy π(a | o): a neural network with 60 inputs, hidden layers [256, 256, 128], 2 outputs. During training it is stochastic (a Gaussian around the network's mean); the judge uses the mean.

Episode: one run from reset to termination. In training: up to 3000 steps (60 s), ending early on a DQ condition. Reset puts the car on the track with a random start.

Return: the discounted sum of future rewards from a step onward. The policy is trained to make the return large.

Gt = rt+1 + γ rt+2 + γ² rt+3 + ...
γ = 0.99 → an effective horizon of about 100 steps, 2 s of driving
maximise Eπ[ G0 ]
over all episodes, all tracks in the batch, all random starts
Discounting is why the agent cares about the next corner more than the lap after next. 2 s is enough to brake for a hairpin; it is not enough to plan a whole lap, so lookahead points in the observation do that job.
Gamma 0.99 gives a time horizon of about 100 steps. Mention that increasing it is possible in config but makes value estimation noisier.

PPO at a high level

Proximal Policy Optimization: collect a batch of experience from every environment, estimate how much better than expected each action was (the advantage), then nudge the policy toward better actions while clipping how far it can move in one update.

PPO is on-policy: the data comes from the current policy and is thrown away after a few epochs. The clipping ratio of 0.2 is what makes it stable enough to run unattended. One iteration is 20 steps on 4096 envs, then 4 epochs over 4 minibatches of 1024.

Why thousands of cars at once

  • One compiled MuJoCo model holds the ground, 600 wall boxes and the car. Tracks are just data: a 400-point centerline and a half-width profile.
  • Placing walls for a different track is a different set of model parameters, not a new compile, so one model serves 4096 environments on 256 tracks in one vectorised step.
  • Physics at 200 Hz, control at 50 Hz, about 80k agent steps per second across the four T4s.
  • The first run of a new shape JIT-compiles for 2 to 4 minutes; the cache under runs/.jax_cache makes later runs instant.
per PPO iteration

Constraint to remember: batch_size × num_minibatches must divide num_envs.
This slide explains why the platform is built the way it is: everything is shaped so that a single compiled model can serve many tracks. Teams only need to know the consequence: huge batches, fast training, a one-time compile cost.

Exploration versus exploitation

The training policy is stochastic: it samples around its mean action. That noise is how it discovers that braking later works, or that cutting a chicane is faster.

An entropy bonus (entropy_cost: 1e-3) keeps the noise from collapsing too early. Too much and the car wobbles forever; too little and it locks into the first thing that worked.

Random starts (position along the lap, lateral and yaw noise) are exploration too: the policy sees every corner from many angles instead of always arriving the same way.

Signs you explore too little

Reward plateaus early. Policy standard deviation drops near zero. The car takes the same line on every track and understeers on new ones.

Signs you explore too much

Reward curve stays noisy. Oscillating steering in replays. Lap times improve slower than the training reward suggests.

The judge uses the deterministic mean action, so a jittery training policy can still race cleanly.
Point teams at train_metrics.jsonl and the dashboard curves: policy entropy and KL per update are logged.

Reward shaping: your lever

r = 1.0 · progress (metres along the centerline this step)
  + 0.0 · speed · dt
  − 0.05 · ‖action − prev_action‖² (smoothness)
  − 0.0 · (lateral / half_width)² (centering, off by default)
  − 0.5 · wall_contact − 20 · off_track − 20 · flipped
  + 0.0 · lap_completed

At 15 m/s the progress term is about 0.3 per step, so the default episode reward is roughly "metres driven" minus penalties.

team/reward.py gets a RewardContext with: progress, speed, lateral, half_width, heading_err, off_track, flipped, wall_contact, car_contact, lap_completed, lap_time, action, prev_action, dt. Pure JAX, runs inside vmap.

The judge never calls it

Reward is training-time only. Lap time is what counts. A reward that is easy to collect without lapping faster will be collected.

Common shaping ideas

  • speed bonus to break slow-and-safe plateaus
  • lap bonus to value finishing
  • heading-error penalty for cleaner lines
  • stronger wall penalty before collision mode
Reward hacking stories are useful here: a policy rewarded for speed alone will spin in circles; a progress reward with no wall penalty will bounce off the walls happily because walls are physical.

Checkpoints and evaluation

  • Training saves a checkpoint at each of the 12 evaluations plus final/: params.pkl (normalizer, policy, value) and meta.json (observation version, network sizes, step).
  • rlr evaluate runs/run1/final --tracks benchmark runs the exact judge: start on the line, 180 s, best lap per track.
  • rlr train --restore DIR warm-starts from a checkpoint, for example to add opponents after solo training.
  • The observation normalizer travels with the checkpoint: inputs that never varied in training (opponent slots when training solo) are left unscaled so they cannot explode later.

Pick the right checkpoint

The last one is not always the fastest. Evaluate several and package the best lap, not the best training reward.

Dashboard

rlr dashboard shows curves, evaluates checkpoints, renders replays and submits, all from a browser over an SSH tunnel.

Mention that a checkpoint from a modified platform is rejected by the judge because the observation version in meta.json will not match.
03

Contest rules

How runs are judged, what disqualifies, what you may change.

Section three: the rules proper.

How a run is judged

  1. The judge unpacks your zip: params.pkl, meta.json, policy.py, car.yaml, submission.json.
  2. It builds your car (clamped) in its copy of the platform and imports your policy.py to get act(params, obs).
  3. For each track: one car, placed on the start line, no noise, 180 s of simulated time.
  4. Score per track = best completed lap. No completed lap = 999 s.
  5. A DQ condition freezes the stats at that moment; laps already completed still count.
deterministicsolo180 s per trackno lap = 999 s
Same code path as rlr evaluate: if it laps in 41 s on your box, it laps in 41 s on the judge.
Allowed imports in policy.py: jax, flax, brax, numpy, mujoco, rlracing. It must be pure JAX: obs shape (60,) in, action shape (2,) in [-1, 1] out.
Emphasise that the judge uses its own copy of rlracing; modifying the platform locally cannot change the judge's physics, and will break the observation-version check.

Scoring

Leaderboard total = sum of best laps over the stage's tracks. Lower is better. A track without a lap adds 999 s. The best submission per team is kept.

A single DNF track costs 999 s, which is more than any lap-time gain elsewhere. Reliability on every track beats brilliance on one.

What ends an episode

Four checks, fixed by the platform: off track beyond half-width + 0.5 m, rolled over (up-axis z < 0.3), faster than 60 m/s, or any non-finite state. Your config can change these numbers for training; the judge ignores your values.

Walls are physical, so "off track" mostly happens by launching over a wall after a collision. Rolling over is the common DQ for cars tuned with a high centre of mass and soft suspension.

Lap validity

  • The centerline is 400 points; the car's position is projected onto it to get its arc length s.
  • A lap completes when s wraps past the start line forwards while the lap is valid.
  • Crossing the line backwards voids the current lap. Driving the track in reverse never scores.
  • In judging the car starts on the line, so the first forward crossing is lap 1.
  • In training with random starts, timing waits for the first crossing of the line.
  • Lap time = steps since the last crossing × 0.02 s.

Multi-car races

Grid slots are behind the line, so every car's first crossing starts its clock. Laps only count while the car is not disqualified. A disqualified car gets zero throttle and coasts to a stop where it is.

The off-track margin is 0.5 m beyond the half-width. Cutting the inside of a corner is fine as long as you stay within it; the walls make that hard anyway.
Same lap logic in the single-car and multi-car simulators. The random-start rule is only relevant in training.

Ghost mode and collision mode

Ghost mode

Every submission's solo run on one track, recorded and replayed together. No contact between cars.

Ranking = solo score on that track. Cars literally drive through each other, so what you see is pure pace.

Stage 1 finale on serpent. Stage 2 primary score on hidden tracks.

Collision mode

All cars in one world with contact on. Grid start in rows of two, 6 m apart, ±1.8 m from the centerline. 240 s, target 3 laps.

Ranking: finishers by time, then still-running cars by laps and progress, then disqualified cars by laps and progress.

Stage 2 finale. Expect a first-corner pile-up from drivers that never saw traffic.

Opponent slots in the observation (indices 45 to 60) are all zero when you train alone. A solo-trained policy sees them light up for the first time in the collision finale. Self-play overnight is how you prepare.
Ghost mode has no interaction, so it is fair and deterministic. Collision mode is the entertainment, with a ranking rule that still rewards finishing.

What you may change

Yours: team/

  • config.yaml: car parameters, car variants, training tracks, start noise, episode length, reward weights, PPO and network hyper-parameters, race block for self-play.
  • reward.py: the reward function, pure JAX.
  • policy.py: how a checkpoint is loaded and turned into an act function.
Only the car block and your policy travel with the submission. Reward, tracks and PPO settings are training-time only.

Frozen: the platform

  • Observation contract v2, 60 floats with fixed scaling. A checkpoint with a different version is rejected.
  • Physics: 200 Hz MuJoCo, 50 Hz control, actions in [-1, 1].
  • Termination and DQ rules, lap logic, judging procedure.
  • Car parameter ranges: out-of-range values are clamped, with a printed note, never rejected.
Do not edit rlracing/. The judge uses its own copy; a mismatch only breaks your own checkpoints.
The contract is enforced technically through the observation version in meta.json, not by trust.

Clamped, never rejected

rlr package writes the clamped values into car.yaml and prints a note for each one it changed.

Trade-offs

More torque and friction mean faster straights and more spins. Lower centre of mass resists rolling over. A longer wheelbase is stable but turns slower; the generator's tightest hidden corners are about 10 m radius.

Wheels add 4 × 20 kg on top of chassis mass, so the default car weighs 530 kg. Changing a range would change physics for everyone, which is why the ranges are frozen.

What the organizer does

A poll loop downloads each new zip, judges it in solo mode on the stage's tracks, writes a result JSON and rebuilds the leaderboard. Restart-safe, so nothing is lost if it hiccups.

Rejections you can avoid

Missing car.yaml, a policy.py that fails to import, or a checkpoint whose observation version is not 2.

The car and what it sees

Geometry, timing, and the 60-float observation, live in 3D.

Section four: the car and the observation contract.

The car

A box chassis on four wheels with independent suspension, built as a MuJoCo model from your 13 parameters.

  • Default footprint 2.91 × 1.82 m, chassis box centred 0.42 m above ground, 530 kg total.
  • Front wheels steer (±30° by default) through position servos; action[0] = −1..1 maps to full lock.
  • Rear-wheel drive by default: 450 N·m per rear wheel; action[1] is the torque fraction. drive_split moves torque to the front.
  • Suspension: a vertical slider per wheel, ±0.12 m travel, spring and damper from your config.
  • Tyres collide with the ground only; the chassis box is what hits walls and other cars.
Walls are 4 × 0.5 × 1 m boxes every 3.2 m along both edges of the track, 600 of them in the model. They are real: hit one and you bounce.
The 3D view is the actual geometry: dimensions come from the same formulas that build the MuJoCo model. Drag to orbit.

Timing and actions

physics dt = 0.005 s (200 Hz)
4 substeps per action
control dt = 0.02 s (50 Hz)
episode ≤ 3000 steps (60 s, training)
judging = 180 s per track (9000 decisions)
action = [steer, throttle] ∈ [-1, 1]²
clipped by the environment, then applied for 4 substeps

steer

−1 full left lock to +1 full right lock, through a servo with your steer_rate. The observation reports the actual front-wheel angle, so the policy can see lag.

throttle

Fraction of max_motor_torque on the driven wheels; negative is braking or reverse. There is no separate brake.

prev_action

The last command is part of the next observation (indices 43 and 44), which is what makes the smoothness penalty learnable.

50 Hz is fast enough for smooth control and slow enough to keep rollouts cheap. 60 m/s is the hard speed limit.

The observation: 60 floats

Eight blocks. Half of the vector is "where is the track going": ten centerline points ahead, in the car frame, plus the width there. Then where you are relative to the centerline, how you are moving, what the wheels do, what you commanded last, and the three nearest cars.

What the car sees

  • lookahead_xy: centerline points, varying m ahead
  • lookahead_half_width: track half-width at those points
  • lateral_heading: offset from centerline, heading error
  • lin_vel: body-frame velocity
  • ang_vel: roll, pitch, yaw rates
  • wheels_steer: wheel spins, steer angle
  • prev_action: last steer, throttle
  • opponents: nearest cars, relative position and velocity
  • all together
Step through the fragments: each one highlights a block in the live scene and dims the rest. The scene is interactive; click into it to orbit or press keys.

Live readout

Right panel shows the scaled values the policy receives, grouped by block, updating live. Point out that every number stays within about ±1, that lookahead x grows with distance, and that opponent slot 3 stays zero because only two other cars exist.

Cheat sheet

Scoring

  • Solo run, 180 s per track
  • Score per track = best lap
  • No lap = 999 s
  • Total = sum over tracks, lower wins
  • Best submission per team counts

DQ

  • Off track > half-width + 0.5 m
  • Rolled over
  • Faster than 60 m/s
  • Non-finite state
  • Backward line crossing voids the lap

Stages

  • Stage 1: benchmark tracks, then ghost race on serpent
  • Overnight: tune the car and retrain
  • Stage 2: hidden tracks, ghost mode is the primary score
  • Finale: collision race, 3 laps, 240 s
