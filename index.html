What you are building

  • A driver: a neural-network policy that reads 60 numbers 50 times a second and outputs steering and throttle.
  • A car setup: 13 tunable physical parameters (wheelbase, tyre friction, torque, ...) that travel with your submission.

02

Reinforcement learning
in five minutes

Just enough vocabulary to read the config file and the training curves.

Section two: RL basics. Keep it practical and tied to the racing setting.

Agent, environment, observation, action, reward

Agent. The driver: a policy π that maps an observation to an action.

Environment. The MJX world: car, track, walls, physics. It owns the true state.

Observation oₜ. What the agent is allowed to see: 60 floats, track- and car-relative.

Action aₜ. Two numbers in [-1, 1]: steer and throttle. Applied for 4 physics substeps (20 ms).

Reward rₜ. A scalar after every step. The agent's only notion of good and bad.

Step. One observe → act → simulate cycle, 50 per simulated second.

The environment keeps the full MuJoCo state; the agent sees only the observation. Reward is a design choice made by the team; lap time is the league's choice and the two are only loosely coupled.

Policy, episode, return, discount

Policy

The driver. A small neural network that turns the 60 observation numbers into steer and throttle. In training it adds a little randomness to explore; the judge runs it without any.

Return

What the policy is trained to maximise: the total reward it expects to collect from now until the end of the episode, not just the reward for the next step.

Episode

One run from reset to the end: up to 60 seconds of driving, or sooner if the car leaves the track, flips or breaks a rule. In training the car starts at a random spot on the track.

Discount

Rewards further in the future count a little less each step. With the default setting the agent effectively looks about 2 seconds ahead: enough to brake for a hairpin, not enough to plan a whole lap. The lookahead points in the observation do that job.

Policy: π(a | o), 60 inputs, hidden layers [256, 256, 128], 2 outputs; stochastic in training (a Gaussian around the network's mean), the judge uses the mean. Episode: up to 3000 steps (60 s), ending early on a DQ condition; reset uses a random start. Return: G_t = r_t+1 + γ r_t+2 + γ² r_t+3 + ..., the discounted sum of future rewards; training maximises the expected return over all episodes, all tracks in the batch and all random starts. Gamma 0.99 gives a time horizon of about 100 steps, 2 s of driving. Mention that increasing it is possible in config but makes value estimation noisier.
How PPO trains the driver
Collect experience from thousands of simulated cars at once, estimate how much better than expected each action was, then take a few careful gradient steps. Repeat.

PPO is on-policy: the data comes from the current policy and is thrown away after a few epochs. The clipping ratio of 0.2 is what makes it stable enough to run unattended. One iteration is 20 steps on 4096 envs, then 4 epochs over 4 minibatches of 1024.

The training policy is stochastic: it samples around its mean action. That noise is how it discovers that braking later works, or that cutting a chicane is faster. An entropy bonus (entropy_cost: 1e-3) keeps the noise from collapsing too early. Too much and the car wobbles forever; too little and it locks into the first thing that worked. Random starts (position along the lap, lateral and yaw noise) are exploration too: the policy sees every corner from many angles instead of always arriving the same way. Signs you explore too little: reward plateaus early, policy standard deviation drops near zero, the car takes the same line on every track and understeers on new ones. Signs you explore too much: reward curve stays noisy, oscillating steering in replays, lap times improve slower than the training reward suggests. The judge uses the deterministic mean action, so a jittery training policy can still race cleanly. Point teams at train_metrics.jsonl and the dashboard curves: policy entropy and KL per update are logged.

The default reward is distance gained along the centerline each step, minus small penalties for jerky inputs and wall contact, and a large penalty for leaving the track or rolling over. At 15 m/s that is about 0.3 per step, so an episode's reward is roughly metres driven. Reward hacking: reward is training-time only; the judge never calls it. Lap time is what counts. A reward that is easy to collect without lapping faster will be collected. Common shaping ideas: a speed bonus to break slow-and-safe plateaus; a lap bonus to value finishing; a heading-error penalty for cleaner lines; a stronger wall penalty before collision mode. Reward hacking stories are useful here: a policy rewarded for speed alone will spin in circles; a progress reward with no wall penalty will bounce off the walls happily because walls are physical. The demo reward is not the default: it adds a speed weight of 0.5 (0.15 per step at 15 m/s) and a centering weight of 0.2, both 0.0 in the baseline config, to show the levers moving. The demo runs two simulated seconds per animation second; the wall scrape is one simulated second, which is 50 steps at −0.5, so −25.

Checkpoints and evaluation

  • Training saves a checkpoint at each of the 12 evaluations plus final/: params.pkl (normalizer, policy, value) and meta.json (observation version, network sizes, step).
  • rlr evaluate runs/run1/final --tracks benchmark runs the exact judge: start on the line, 180 s, best lap per track.
  • rlr train --restore DIR warm-starts from a checkpoint, for example to add opponents after solo training.
  • The observation normalizer travels with the checkpoint: inputs that never varied in training (opponent slots when training solo) are left unscaled so they cannot explode later.

Pick the right checkpoint

The last one is not always the fastest. Evaluate several and package the best lap, not the best training reward.

Dashboard

rlr dashboard shows curves, evaluates checkpoints, renders replays and submits, all from a browser over an SSH tunnel.

Mention that a checkpoint from a modified platform is rejected by the judge because the observation version in meta.json will not match.

What ends an episode (and disqualifies a judged run)
Fixed by the platform. The same four checks apply in training, evaluation and judging.
Off the track
|lateral offset| > half-width + 0.5 m. Walls normally prevent this.
Rolled over
The car's up axis has z < 0.3: it is on its side or roof.
Too fast
Speed above 60 m/s. Guards against solver blow-ups.
Non-finite state
Any NaN or inf in position or velocity ends the run.
Lap validity. A lap counts only when you cross the line forwards after a valid start. Crossing it backwards voids the lap. Random training starts wait for the first crossing before timing. In judging you start on the line, so lap 1 counts.
In multi-car races a DQ'd car coasts to a stop with zero throttle and keeps the laps it had.
Walls are physical, so "off track" mostly happens by launching over a wall after a collision. Rolling over is the common DQ for cars tuned with a high centre of mass and soft suspension.

The centerline is 400 points; the car's position is projected onto it to get its arc length s. A lap completes when s wraps past the start line forwards while the lap is valid. Crossing the line backwards voids the current lap. Driving the track in reverse never scores. In judging the car starts on the line, so the first forward crossing is lap 1. In training with random starts, timing waits for the first crossing of the line. Lap time = steps since the last crossing × 0.02 s. Multi-car races: grid slots are behind the line, so every car's first crossing starts its clock. Laps only count while the car is not disqualified. A disqualified car gets zero throttle and coasts to a stop where it is. The off-track margin is 0.5 m beyond the half-width. Cutting the inside of a corner is fine as long as you stay within it; the walls make that hard anyway. Same lap logic in the single-car and multi-car simulators. The random-start rule is only relevant in training. The demo lap clock runs six times faster than the animation.

Ghost mode and collision mode

Ghost modesolo runs, replayed together

Every car starts alone on the line; the solo replays are overlaid, so they begin stacked, spread out by pace, and pass through each other. Ranking is each car's own best lap.

Collision modeone world, contact on

Grid start, first to 3 laps in 240 s. Finishers rank by time, then running cars by laps and progress, then DQs. A DQ'd car coasts to a stop.

Ghost mode has no interaction, so it is fair and deterministic. Collision mode is the entertainment, with a ranking rule that still rewards finishing.

What you may change

Yours: team/

  • config.yaml: car parameters, car variants, training tracks, start noise, episode length, reward weights, PPO and network hyper-parameters, race block for self-play.
  • reward.py: the reward function, pure JAX.
  • policy.py: how a checkpoint is loaded and turned into an act function.
Only the car block and your policy travel with the submission. Reward, tracks and PPO settings are training-time only.

Frozen: the platform

  • Observation contract v2, 60 floats with fixed scaling. A checkpoint with a different version is rejected.
  • Physics: 200 Hz MuJoCo, 50 Hz control, actions in [-1, 1].
  • Termination and DQ rules, lap logic, judging procedure.
  • Car parameter ranges: out-of-range values are clamped, with a printed note, never rejected.
Do not edit rlracing/. The judge uses its own copy; a mismatch only breaks your own checkpoints.
The contract is enforced technically through the observation version in meta.json, not by trust.

A box chassis on four wheels with independent suspension, built as a MuJoCo model from your 13 parameters. Default footprint 2.91 × 1.82 m, chassis box centred 0.42 m above ground, 530 kg total. Front wheels steer (±30° by default) through position servos; action[0] = −1..1 maps to full lock. Rear-wheel drive by default: 450 N·m per rear wheel; action[1] is the torque fraction. drive_split moves torque to the front. Suspension: a vertical slider per wheel, ±0.12 m travel, spring and damper from your config. Tyres collide with the ground only; the chassis box is what hits walls and other cars. Walls are 4 × 0.5 × 1 m boxes every 3.2 m along both edges of the track, 600 of them in the model. They are real: hit one and you bounce. The 3D view is the actual geometry: dimensions come from the same formulas that build the MuJoCo model. Drag to orbit. Ranges (min / default / max): wheelbase 1.6 / 2.4 / 3.2 m. track_width 1.1 / 1.5 / 2.0 m. wheel_radius 0.22 / 0.32 / 0.42 m. wheel_width 0.15 / 0.22 / 0.35 m. chassis_mass 250 / 450 / 900 kg. com_height 0.30 / 0.42 / 0.70 m. suspension_stiffness 10 000 / 35 000 / 80 000 N/m. suspension_damping 500 / 3 000 / 8 000 N·s/m. max_steer_deg 15 / 30 / 40 deg. steer_rate 2.0 / 6.0 / 12.0 rad/s. max_motor_torque 300 / 900 / 1 600 N·m total. drive_split 0.0 / 0.0 / 1.0 (0 = RWD, 1 = FWD). tire_friction 0.7 / 1.1 / 1.4. Plus wheel_collision: cylinder (default) or sphere; spheres are cheaper and smoother, cylinders have a flat contact patch. Values are clamped, never rejected: rlr package writes the clamped values into car.yaml and prints a note for each one it changed. Wheels add 4 × 20 kg on top of chassis mass, so the default car weighs 530 kg. Changing a range would change physics for everyone, which is why the ranges are frozen.

Timing: physics dt = 0.005 s (200 Hz); 4 substeps per action; control dt = 0.02 s (50 Hz); an episode is at most 3000 steps (60 s, training); judging is 180 s per track, 9000 decisions. action = [steer, throttle] in [-1, 1]², clipped by the environment, then applied for 4 substeps. Steer: +1 is full left lock, -1 full right lock (the steer hinge turns about +z, so positive is positive yaw), through a servo with your steer_rate. The observation reports the actual front-wheel angle, so the policy can see lag. Throttle: fraction of max_motor_torque on the driven wheels; negative is braking or reverse. There is no separate brake. prev_action: the last command is part of the next observation (indices 43 and 44), which is what makes the smoothness penalty learnable. 50 Hz is fast enough for smooth control and slow enough to keep rollouts cheap. 60 m/s is the hard speed limit. The demo replays a scripted 50 Hz action sequence at about one sixth speed on the parked car: red tyre glow is forward torque, blue is braking or reverse, and the dashed needle is the command while the solid one is the wheel angle.
Observation v2: the 60 numbers the policy sees
Fixed contract. Every entry is divided by a constant so it is O(1); a checkpoint trained against a different version is rejected.
Policy contract: act(params, obs[60]) → action[2] in [-1, 1], pure JAX.
Eight blocks. Half of the vector is "where is the track going": ten centerline points ahead, in the car frame, plus the width there. Then where you are relative to the centerline, how you are moving, what the wheels do, what you commanded last, and the three nearest cars.

What the car sees

  • lookahead_xy: centerline points, varying m ahead
  • lookahead_half_width: track half-width at those points
  • lateral_heading: offset from centerline, heading error
  • lin_vel: body-frame velocity
  • ang_vel: roll, pitch, yaw rates
  • wheels_steer: wheel spins, steer angle
  • prev_action: last steer, throttle
  • opponents: nearest cars, relative position and velocity
  • all together
Step through the fragments: each one highlights a block in the live scene and dims the rest. The scene is interactive; click into it to orbit or press keys.

Cheat sheet

Scoring

  • Solo run, 180 s per track
  • Score per track = best lap
  • No lap = 999 s
  • Total = sum over tracks, lower wins
  • Best submission per team counts

DQ

  • Off track > half-width + 0.5 m
  • Rolled over
  • Faster than 60 m/s
  • Non-finite state
  • Backward line crossing voids the lap

Stages

  • Stage 1: benchmark tracks, then ghost race on serpent
  • Overnight: tune the car and retrain
  • Stage 2: hidden tracks, ghost mode is the primary score
  • Finale: collision race, 3 laps, 240 s
